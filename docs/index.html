Galley Catchment Study

How many RYC memberships are close enough to the club to come in for dinner? This study uses the methods retailers use to decide whether a location is worth investing in (drive-time trade areas, distance decay and demand estimates) to help the board evaluate an expansion of the Galley.

Dinner demand model

A distance-decay model, as retailers use to forecast store traffic. Memberships close to the club are most likely to come to dinner, and the likelihood falls with each extra 15 minutes of driving. The default values are placeholders. Calibrate them with the Galley's actual dinner counts before drawing conclusions.

Where the diners come from

Formula: diners = Σ over bands of memberships × dinner rate × (1 − drop-off)band index × diners per membership. Only memberships within 60 minutes are counted. The traffic factor stretches every drive time, which moves memberships into slower bands; anything pushed past 60 minutes drops out.

Next steps: data for a complete analysis

This study shows where memberships are and how far they are from the club. To decide whether a bigger Galley pays for itself, the board also needs to know how members actually use the Galley today and what an expansion would cost. These are the datasets that would close that gap, most important first.

Analysis these data would enable

  • A calibrated demand model: fit the dinner rate and drop-off to real attendance by drive-time band, so the covers estimate is measured rather than assumed.
  • A break-even and payback estimate: added covers × average check × margin, compared with the expansion cost.
  • A map of regular diners: where frequent Galley users live compared with all memberships, aggregated to protect privacy as on this page.
  • Dinner-hour drive times: traffic-aware routing for a typical 6 pm weekday arrival, instead of the no-traffic times used here.
  • Richer spatial layers: for example Google Earth Engine surfaces combining drive time, membership density and attendance on one map.

Methodology

1. Membership data

  • The source is the club's roster of active Regular and Associate memberships. Each row is one membership, which may cover a household. Only three fields are used: zip code, the age on the membership, and membership type. No names, street addresses or contact details are processed.
  • ZIP+4 codes (e.g. 94510-1234) are shortened to 5 digits. PO-box-only zips have no Census boundary, so each is assigned to the zip that contains its post office (e.g. 94807 → 94801 Richmond).
  • Ages that are blank or under 18 are treated as not recorded and left out of all age statistics; one record had an age of 0. Those memberships still count everywhere else.

2. Drive time and distance

  • Per-zip drive time and road distance come from the openrouteservice Matrix API, using the driving-car profile on the OpenStreetMap road network. Each route runs from the clubhouse to a representative point of the zip: a point guaranteed to be inside the zip's boundary, unlike a geometric centroid, which can land in the Bay.
  • Zips are placed in bands by routed time: 0–15, 15–30, 30–45, 45–60, or more than 60 minutes. Upper limits are inclusive, so a 15.0-minute zip is in the 0–15 band.
  • The drive-time rings on Map 1 are built from the same routed zip times. For each band, all zips within that time are merged into one area, and small gaps are closed (0.8 km buffer) so the outline reads cleanly. A zip inside the "15 min" ring is therefore exactly a zip in the 0–15 minute band. openrouteservice isochrones set the study-area extent and are the backup method for any zip that can't be routed.
  • Travel times assume typical free-flow speeds with no live traffic. The traffic factor in the demand model shows how sensitive the results are to congestion.

3. Geography and cartography

  • Zip boundaries are 2020 Census ZCTAs (Zip Code Tabulation Areas), which approximate USPS zip codes. Counties are 2020 Census boundaries, assigned by each zip's representative point.
  • Area calculations use NAD83 / California Albers (EPSG:3310), an equal-area projection. The maps are displayed in Web Mercator (EPSG:3857), as usual for web maps; north is up everywhere.
  • Boundaries are simplified to a 40 m tolerance so the page loads quickly. Map 1 shades each zip by its membership count in five classes (1, 2–4, 5–9, 10–19, 20 or more). The classes were picked by hand so most zips, which have only a few memberships, still show up next to the club's own zip, which has 102.

4. Privacy

  • Only aggregated counts are published. For any zip with fewer than 5 memberships, the median age and Regular/Associate split are hidden, so no individual's age can be worked out. The membership count itself is still shown.
  • In the age chart, any cell with fewer than 5 memberships is also hidden.
  • The raw roster never leaves the analyst's computer and is not part of the published site or code repository.

5. Demand model

  • A banded distance-decay model: attendance likelihood falls by a fixed share with each 15-minute band, a simplified form of the Huff and gravity models used in retail site selection. The inputs are assumptions, not measurements, until they are fitted to real Galley attendance.

6. Limitations

  • A zip's memberships are treated as living at one point, so large rural zips are the least precise.
  • Drive times assume no traffic, and the demand model's inputs are placeholders. See Next steps for the data that would replace these assumptions with measurements.
